My 10% funding-rate strategy loses money after trading costs

By Harry Winter · 1 November 2026 · From the polymarket-edge project

A Hyperliquid funding strategy made +10% a year out-of-sample and was positive in every test window. With realistic trading costs and 8-hourly rebalancing it lost 207% a year. Here's what made it work again.

On crypto perpetual futures exchanges like Hyperliquid, traders on one side pay the other a “funding rate” every hour. When it’s positive, people who are short get paid. So a simple idea: short the coins with the highest funding, hedge the price risk, and collect the payments.

I backtested this as part of polymarket-edge on 12 major coins over a year of hourly data: 105,120 funding ticks.

The headline looked great

Every 8 hours, the strategy ranks coins by their average funding over the last day and shorts the top 5. Before costs:

  • +11.09% a year in-sample and +10.06% out-of-sample, so very little decay
  • Positive in 20 of 20 walk-forward test windows
  • A block-bootstrap 95% confidence interval of +9.78% to +13.59%, comfortably above zero

I used a block bootstrap rather than a plain one because funding payments are autocorrelated. The plain version gave a much tighter, over-confident interval.

Then I added trading costs

Rebalancing means trading the short and its hedge each time. At a realistic 5 basis points per leg, rebalancing every 8 hours turns +10% into −207% a year. The costs are paid three times a day; the funding edge isn’t big enough to cover them.

The fix is to trade less. Rebalancing every two weeks brings it back to +3.85% a year after costs, and monthly gives +4.87%. The edge is real, but small, and it only survives if you’re patient.

Things that didn’t hold up

  • “20 of 20 windows” overstates it. The test windows overlap, so it’s closer to 10 independent tests.
  • About 7.7 percentage points of the return is just the baseline funding rate any short collects. Picking coins only added about 3.8 points.
  • Ranking coins by how fast funding was rising instead of how high it was did worse: +8.0% versus +17.2% on the same data.

What I took from it

A backtest without costs tells you whether a signal exists, not whether you can make money from it. Most of the useful work here was finding out why the good-looking number was wrong. The code is on GitHub.
