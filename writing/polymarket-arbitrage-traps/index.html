Most of the Polymarket arbitrage I found was a trap

By Harry Winter · 1 November 2026 · From the polymarket-edge project

I scanned 500 Polymarket events for arbitrage. 12 of the 19 signals were traps that lose money at just $50 a market, and every one of them was a US politics or election market.

Polymarket has events with several mutually exclusive outcomes, like “who wins the World Cup”. Exactly one outcome resolves YES, so the YES prices across all of them should add up to $1. When they don’t, it looks like free money: buy or sell the whole basket and lock in the difference.

I built a scanner for this as part of polymarket-edge. The first version only looked at the best price on each market. It found plenty of gaps. Then I checked whether you could actually trade them.

How I tested it

I pulled 500 active events and flagged any whose prices were more than 0.5% (50 basis points) away from $1, which is roughly where fees stop eating the profit. 19 events were flagged. For each one I walked the full order book on every market, and worked out what the gap would really be if you traded $50 per market, and then $500 per market.

  • Real: the gap is still above 0.5% at $500 per market.
  • Marginal: still positive at $50, but gone by $500.
  • Trap: the gap turns into a loss at just $50 per market, because at least one market has almost no one on the other side.

What I found

Of the 19 flagged events, 2 were real, 5 were marginal and 12 were traps. That’s a 63% trap rate.

The traps weren’t random. Almost every flagged US politics or election event was a trap: 11 of 13. These tend to be two-market state races with very lopsided prices, where the cheap side has almost no depth. They produce the biggest-looking gaps, and they’re the ones you can’t trade.

The two real signals were the 48-market 2026 FIFA World Cup winner event and the 20-market Nobel Peace Prize event. Both have liquidity spread across lots of markets, so the gap survives when you trade at size. On the World Cup event, the gap held up through roughly $48,000 of total basket size.

Counting by dollars changes the story

63% sounds bad. But if you weight each flagged event by how much money is actually traded on it, the trap share drops to about 0.012%. The World Cup event alone carried 95.9% of the flagged volume. So most signals are traps, but most of the money is in a signal that’s real.

What I took from it

  • A price-only scanner is good at finding candidates and bad at finding trades. Walking the order book isn’t optional.
  • A gap on a two-market US state election is almost certainly a trap.
  • How you count matters. “63% traps” and “0.012% traps” are the same data.

This was one snapshot, and order books move hourly, so the exact 63% will drift. The pattern, that thin two-market political events are where the traps live, looks structural. The code and the full write-up are on GitHub.
